# 50 Fundamental Equations of Algebra

Welcome to the Accessible Algebra Reference Guide. This resource is designed to provide blind, visually impaired, and sighted students with an intuitive, screen-reader-friendly reference of 50 core algebraic equations. Every equation features plain English explanations, clean MathML markup, and in-place interactive sub-expression navigation.

---

## 1. Slope-Intercept Form of a Linear Equation
$$y = mx + b$$
This equation describes a straight line on a two-dimensional grid. The variable $m$ represents the slope (how steep the line is), and $b$ represents the y-intercept (the exact point where the line crosses the vertical y-axis). By plugging in any x-value, you can immediately calculate the corresponding y-value on the line.

---

## 2. Point-Slope Form of a Linear Equation
$$y - y_1 = m(x - x_1)$$
This formula helps you write the equation of a line when you know its slope $m$ and one specific point $(x_1, y_1)$ on the line. It states that the change in the vertical direction divided by the change in the horizontal direction remains constant everywhere on the line. It is commonly used when finding tangent lines in geometry and calculus.

---

## 3. Standard Form of a Linear Equation
$$Ax + By = C$$
This is the general form used to express linear relationships with integer coefficients where $A$, $B$, and $C$ are real numbers. It is particularly useful for quickly finding both the x-intercept (by setting $y = 0$) and the y-intercept (by setting $x = 0$). It also simplifies working with systems of linear equations in algebra and matrices.

---

## 4. Slope Formula Between Two Points
$$m = \frac{y_2 - y_1}{x_2 - x_1}$$
The slope $m$ measures the rate of vertical change (rise) compared to horizontal change (run) between two points $(x_1, y_1)$ and $(x_2, y_2)$. A positive slope indicates the line rises from left to right, while a negative slope falls. If the denominator equals zero, the line is vertical and the slope is undefined.

---

## 5. Midpoint Formula in Two Dimensions
$$M = \left(\frac{x_1 + x_2}{2}, \frac{y_1 + y_2}{2}\right)$$
This formula calculates the exact middle point between two given points on a coordinate plane. It works by taking the arithmetic average of the two x-coordinates and the arithmetic average of the two y-coordinates. It is essential in geometry for bisecting line segments and finding centers of circles.

---

## 6. Distance Formula in the Cartesian Plane
$$d = \sqrt{(x_2 - x_1)^2 + (y_2 - y_1)^2}$$
This equation calculates the straight-line distance $d$ between two points in a two-dimensional coordinate system. It is derived directly from the Pythagorean theorem by treating the horizontal and vertical differences as the legs of a right triangle. The result is always a non-negative number.

---

## 7. Two-Variable Linear System Solution by Cramer's Rule
$$x = \frac{c_1 b_2 - c_2 b_1}{a_1 b_2 - a_2 b_1}$$
Cramer's Rule uses determinants to solve systems of linear equations directly without elimination or substitution. In this formula, the numerator is the determinant formed by replacing the x-column with constants, and the denominator is the coefficient determinant. The system has a unique solution as long as the denominator is not zero.

---

## 8. Definition of Absolute Value
$$|x| = \sqrt{x^2}$$
The absolute value represents the geometric distance of a number $x$ from zero on the number line, disregarding its direction. Because distance cannot be negative, squaring $x$ makes it positive, and taking the principal square root yields the positive magnitude. For positive numbers, $|x| = x$; for negative numbers, $|x| = -x$.

---

## 9. Absolute Value Inequality
$$|x - a| \le b \iff a - b \le x \le a + b$$
This inequality states that the distance between a variable $x$ and a fixed center point $a$ is at most $b$ units. It establishes a closed interval symmetric around $a$ with a total width of $2b$. This formulation is widely used in statistics for confidence intervals and in calculus for epsilon-delta proofs.

---

## 10. Triangle Inequality
$$|a + b| \le |a| + |b|$$
The triangle inequality states that the length of the sum of two quantities cannot exceed the sum of their individual lengths. Geometrically, in any triangle, the length of one side is always less than or equal to the sum of the other two sides. It is a fundamental property of distances in real numbers, complex numbers, and vector spaces.

---

## 11. Standard Form of a Quadratic Equation
$$ax^2 + bx + c = 0$$
This is the standard algebraic formulation for a second-degree polynomial equation where $a \ne 0$. The graph of this function creates a smooth U-shaped curve called a parabola. The values of $x$ that satisfy this equation are called the roots, zeros, or x-intercepts of the parabola.

---

## 12. The Quadratic Formula
$$x = \frac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a}$$
The quadratic formula provides an exact method to find the roots of any quadratic equation $ax^2 + bx + c = 0$. The plus-or-minus symbol $\pm$ indicates that quadratic equations typically have two solutions. It is derived by completing the square on the general quadratic equation.

---

## 13. Discriminant of a Quadratic Polynomial
$$\Delta = b^2 - 4ac$$
The discriminant $\Delta$ (delta) is the expression under the square root in the quadratic formula. If $\Delta > 0$, the equation has two distinct real roots; if $\Delta = 0$, there is exactly one repeated real root. If $\Delta < 0$, the equation has two complex conjugate roots with no real x-intercepts.

---

## 14. Vertex Form of a Parabola
$$y = a(x - h)^2 + k$$
The vertex form reveals the peak or lowest point of a parabola at the coordinates $(h, k)$ by inspection. The parameter $a$ determines whether the parabola opens upward ($a > 0$) or downward ($a < 0$) and controls how wide or narrow the curve is. It is formed by completing the square on the standard quadratic form.

---

## 15. Vertex Coordinates Formula
$$h = -\frac{b}{2a}, \quad k = c - \frac{b^2}{4a}$$
These formulas calculate the horizontal center $h$ and vertical peak or trough $k$ of a parabola directly from standard coefficients $a$, $b$, and $c$. The vertical line $x = h$ is the axis of symmetry that divides the parabola into two mirror-image halves. Finding the vertex is the standard method for solving quadratic optimization problems.

---

## 16. Difference of Squares
$$a^2 - b^2 = (a - b)(a + b)$$
This factoring identity states that subtracting one squared quantity from another factors into the product of their difference and their sum. It simplifies polynomial division and allows rapid mental arithmetic, such as calculating $49 \times 51 = (50 - 1)(50 + 1) = 2500 - 1 = 2499$. It is also used to rationalize denominators with radicals.

---

## 17. Square of a Binomial Sum
$$(a + b)^2 = a^2 + 2ab + b^2$$
Expanding the square of a sum produces three terms: the first term squared, twice the product of both terms, and the second term squared. The middle term $2ab$ accounts for the cross-multiplications when applying the distributive property. Forgetting this middle term is one of the most common algebra errors.

---

## 18. Square of a Binomial Difference
$$(a - b)^2 = a^2 - 2ab + b^2$$
Expanding the square of a difference yields the square of the first term, minus twice their product, plus the square of the second term. Notice that the final term $b^2$ is always positive because squaring any real negative number produces a positive result. This identity is the foundation for completing the square.

---

## 19. Sum of Two Cubes
$$a^3 + b^3 = (a + b)(a^2 - ab + b^2)$$
This special algebraic identity allows you to factor the sum of two cubed terms into a linear factor $(a + b)$ and a quadratic factor $(a^2 - ab + b^2)$. The sign in the linear factor matches the original plus sign, while the middle term of the quadratic factor has the opposite (minus) sign. The quadratic factor cannot be factored further over real numbers.

---

## 20. Difference of Two Cubes
$$a^3 - b^3 = (a - b)(a^2 + ab + b^2)$$
This formula factors the difference between two cubic terms into a binomial difference $(a - b)$ multiplied by a trinomial $(a^2 + ab + b^2)$. Notice that all terms inside the second trinomial factor are positive. It is widely used when simplifying complex fractions and evaluating calculus limits involving cube roots.

---

## 21. Product Rule for Exponents
$$a^m \cdot a^n = a^{m + n}$$
When multiplying powers that have the identical base $a$, you keep the base unchanged and add their exponent powers together. For example, $x^3 \cdot x^4 = (x \cdot x \cdot x) \cdot (x \cdot x \cdot x \cdot x) = x^7$. This fundamental law holds true for all integers, fractions, and real exponent values.

---

## 22. Quotient Rule for Exponents
$$\frac{a^m}{a^n} = a^{m - n}$$
When dividing two exponential expressions with the same base $a$, you keep the base and subtract the bottom exponent from the top exponent. This rule requires that the base $a$ is not zero. It naturally leads to the rule that any non-zero number raised to the power of zero equals one ($a^0 = a^{n-n} = 1$).

---

## 23. Power of a Power Rule
$$(a^m)^n = a^{m n}$$
When raising an exponential term to another exponent, you multiply the powers together while keeping the base the same. For instance, $(x^2)^3 = x^2 \cdot x^2 \cdot x^2 = x^6$. This property is critical when converting higher-order radical equations into solvable polynomial forms.

---

## 24. Negative Exponent Definition
$$a^{-n} = \frac{1}{a^n}$$
A negative exponent indicates a reciprocal rather than a negative number value. Raising a base $a$ to the power $-n$ means dividing 1 by the base multiplied by itself $n$ times. For example, $2^{-3} = \frac{1}{2^3} = \frac{1}{8}$.

---

## 25. Fractional Exponent and Radical Equivalence
$$a^{\frac{m}{n}} = \sqrt[n]{a^m}$$
Fractional exponents represent roots: the denominator $n$ specifies the index of the radical (such as square root or cube root), while the numerator $m$ represents the power. Writing radicals as fractional exponents enables the use of standard exponent rules to simplify complex radical expressions.

---

## 26. Product Rule for Radicals
$$\sqrt[n]{a b} = \sqrt[n]{a} \cdot \sqrt[n]{b}$$
The nth root of a product equals the product of the individual nth roots of each factor. This rule allows you to simplify radicals by factoring out perfect squares, cubes, or higher powers (for example, $\sqrt{50} = \sqrt{25 \cdot 2} = 5\sqrt{2}$). For even roots, both $a$ and $b$ must be non-negative real numbers.

---

## 27. Quotient Rule for Radicals
$$\sqrt[n]{\frac{a}{b}} = \frac{\sqrt[n]{a}}{\sqrt[n]{b}}$$
The nth root of a fraction equals the nth root of the numerator divided by the nth root of the denominator. This allows you to simplify fractions inside radicals independently and is the first step in rationalizing denominators. The denominator $b$ must be strictly positive.

---

## 28. Binomial Theorem Expansion
$$(x + y)^n = \sum_{k=0}^{n} \binom{n}{k} x^{n-k} y^k$$
The Binomial Theorem provides an algebraic formula for expanding any binomial $(x + y)$ raised to an integer power $n$ without multiplying term by term. The coefficients $\binom{n}{k}$ correspond exactly to the entries in row $n$ of Pascal's Triangle. The powers of $x$ decrease from $n$ to $0$ while the powers of $y$ increase from $0$ to $n$.

---

## 29. Binomial Coefficient Combination Formula
$$\binom{n}{k} = \frac{n!}{k!(n - k)!}$$
This formula calculates the number of ways to choose $k$ items from a set of $n$ distinct items without regard to order. The exclamation mark indicates a factorial (multiplying all positive integers down to 1). In algebra, these numbers form the coefficients of binomial expansions and probability distributions.

---

## 30. Vieta's Formulas for a Quadratic Equation
$$r_1 + r_2 = -\frac{b}{a}, \quad r_1 r_2 = \frac{c}{a}$$
Vieta's formulas establish a direct relationship between the roots ($r_1, r_2$) of a polynomial and its coefficients without solving the equation explicitly. The sum of the roots equals the negative of the linear coefficient divided by the leading coefficient, while the product equals the constant term divided by the leading coefficient.

---

## 31. Fundamental Definition of a Logarithm
$$\log_b(x) = y \iff b^y = x$$
A logarithm answers the question: "To what power must I raise base $b$ to obtain the value $x$?" The base $b$ must be positive and not equal to 1, and $x$ must be strictly positive. Logarithms are the inverse operations of exponential functions.

---

## 32. Product Property of Logarithms
$$\log_b(x y) = \log_b(x) + \log_b(y)$$
The logarithm of a product equals the sum of the individual logarithms of its factors. This property transforms complex multiplication operations into simpler addition steps, which formed the foundation of historical slide rules and scientific computation.

---

## 33. Quotient Property of Logarithms
$$\log_b\left(\frac{x}{y}\right) = \log_b(x) - \log_b(y)$$
The logarithm of a quotient equals the logarithm of the numerator minus the logarithm of the denominator. It converts division into subtraction, making it possible to simplify complicated algebraic fractions before differentiating or evaluating them.

---

## 34. Power Property of Logarithms
$$\log_b(x^p) = p \log_b(x)$$
This property allows an exponent $p$ inside a logarithm to be moved out front as a multiplicative coefficient. It is the primary tool used to solve exponential equations where the unknown variable is located in the exponent.

---

## 35. Change of Base Formula for Logarithms
$$\log_b(x) = \frac{\ln(x)}{\ln(b)}$$
The change of base formula allows you to calculate logarithms of any arbitrary base $b$ using standard natural logarithms ($\ln$) or base-10 logarithms. It is invaluable when evaluating logarithmic expressions on calculators and programming languages that only provide built-in natural logs.

---

## 36. Continuous Exponential Growth and Decay Model
$$A(t) = A_0 e^{k t}$$
This equation models quantities that change at a rate proportional to their current size, such as population growth or radioactive decay. $A_0$ is the initial amount at time $t = 0$, $e$ is Euler's constant, and $k$ is the continuous growth rate ($k > 0$) or decay rate ($k < 0$).

---

## 37. Compound Interest Formula
$$A = P \left(1 + \frac{r}{n}\right)^{n t}$$
This formula calculates the accumulated future value $A$ of an initial principal investment $P$ after $t$ years. The variable $r$ is the annual interest rate, and $n$ is the number of times interest is compounded per year (e.g., $n = 12$ for monthly compounding). Interest is earned on both the initial principal and accumulated interest over time.

---

## 38. Continuously Compounded Interest
$$A = P e^{r t}$$
This formula calculates the growth of an investment $P$ when interest is compounded continuously at every infinitesimal instant of time. It is the mathematical limit of the compound interest formula as the compounding frequency $n$ approaches infinity. It provides the maximum possible interest return for a given annual rate $r$.

---

## 39. nth Term of an Arithmetic Sequence
$$a_n = a_1 + (n - 1)d$$
An arithmetic sequence increases or decreases by adding a constant common difference $d$ at each step. This equation allows you to determine the value of any $n$-th term directly from the starting term $a_1$ without calculating all intervening terms.

---

## 40. Sum of the First n Terms of an Arithmetic Series
$$S_n = \frac{n}{2}(a_1 + a_n)$$
This formula gives the total sum of the first $n$ terms of an arithmetic progression. Intuitively, it multiplies the number of terms $n$ by the average of the first and last terms. A famous legend attributes this insight to young Carl Friedrich Gauss pairing numbers from opposite ends of a sequence.

---

## 41. nth Term of a Geometric Sequence
$$a_n = a_1 r^{n-1}$$
In a geometric sequence, each term is generated by multiplying the preceding term by a constant common ratio $r$. This equation calculates the $n$-th term directly from the initial term $a_1$ and the ratio $r$. Geometric sequences grow exponentially when $|r| > 1$.

---

## 42. Sum of a Finite Geometric Series
$$S_n = \frac{a_1 (1 - r^n)}{1 - r}$$
This formula computes the exact sum of the first $n$ terms of a geometric series where $r \ne 1$. It is used across economics and engineering to calculate amortized loan payments, annuities, and digital signal accumulations.

---

## 43. Sum of an Infinite Convergent Geometric Series
$$S_\infty = \frac{a}{1 - r}$$
When the common ratio $r$ has an absolute value strictly less than 1 ($|r| < 1$), an infinite number of terms sum to a finite value. As more terms are added, each subsequent term shrinks toward zero. This formula is fundamental for converting repeating decimals into exact fractions.

---

## 44. Standard Form of a Complex Number
$$z = a + b i$$
A complex number $z$ consists of a real part $a$ and an imaginary part $b$, where $i$ is defined by the property $i^2 = -1$. Complex numbers are represented geometrically as points $(a, b)$ on a two-dimensional complex plane with horizontal real and vertical imaginary axes.

---

## 45. Modulus of a Complex Number
$$|z| = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2}$$
The modulus (or magnitude) $|z|$ measures the Euclidean distance from the origin $(0, 0)$ to the complex number $z = a + bi$ in the complex plane. It is always a non-negative real number and is the complex equivalent of the absolute value.

---

## 46. Polar Form of a Complex Number
$$z = r (\cos \theta + i \sin \theta)$$
Polar form expresses a complex number by its distance from the origin $r = |z|$ and its angle $\theta$ (the argument) measured counterclockwise from the positive real axis. This representation turns complex multiplication into multiplying magnitudes and adding angles.

---

## 47. De Moivre's Formula for Complex Powers
$$[r (\cos \theta + i \sin \theta)]^n = r^n (\cos(n\theta) + i \sin(n\theta))$$
De Moivre's formula provides a rapid way to raise any complex number to an integer power $n$. Instead of expanding binomials repeatedly, you raise the modulus $r$ to the $n$-th power and multiply the angle $\theta$ by $n$. It is also used to find the $n$ distinct roots of complex numbers.

---

## 48. Determinant of a Two-by-Two Matrix
$$\det \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix} = ad - bc$$
The determinant of a 2x2 matrix is computed by subtracting the product of the off-diagonal elements ($bc$) from the product of the main diagonal elements ($ad$). Geometrically, its absolute value represents the area of the parallelogram formed by the matrix's column vectors. If the determinant is zero, the matrix cannot be inverted.

---

## 49. Inverse of a Two-by-Two Matrix
$$\begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix}^{-1} = \frac{1}{ad - bc} \begin{pmatrix} d & -b \\ -c & a \end{pmatrix}$$
The inverse matrix reverses the linear transformation performed by the original matrix. You swap the elements on the main diagonal ($a$ and $d$), negate the off-diagonal elements ($b$ and $c$), and divide every element by the determinant $(ad - bc)$. If $ad - bc = 0$, the inverse does not exist.

---

## 50. Euler's Identity
$$e^{i \pi} + 1 = 0$$
Euler's Identity connects the five most fundamental mathematical constants in a single elegant equation: $e$ (the base of natural logarithms), $i$ (the imaginary unit), $\pi$ (the ratio of a circle's circumference to its diameter), 1 (the multiplicative identity), and 0 (the additive identity). It is a special case of Euler's formula $e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta + i\sin\theta$ evaluated at $\theta = \pi$.
